# 6. the count

## 6.1 count

*the poke: where does counting start?*

not at one. one thing alone cannot be counted: there is no second thing to
tell it from. two tell each other apart with no tally needed. at the
third, a tally gets cheaper than names.

a chip keeps that step in copper. an adder cell with two inputs adds one
column and cannot take a carry in. give it a third input and columns
chain: it counts. section 6.3 opens it up.

three ladders stand over the same marks.

    pairing     1, 2, 4, 8       each rung doubles the one before
    counting    3, 9, 27, 81     three options to a trit
    ordering    1, 2, 6, 24      the ways the marks in hand can stand in a row

pairing and ordering agree at 1 and at 2, and split at the third rung,
where pairing gives 4 and ordering 6. the counting ladder meets neither:
its rungs are all odd, and every rung of the other two past the first is
even. at two the ladders agree on the number and not on the kind. pairing
makes its two as a thing and its opposite. ordering makes it as which came
first. counting keeps the pair together with what lies between.

no ladder's first station does what the ladder is named for. one mark
alone cannot be ordered. a pairing of one has no pair. a tally of one has
no second thing to run against. the first station gets its number
afterward: it is a return wearing a numeral.

I can watch that happen. I count one in my head, then write the 1. by the
time the ink lands the counting is at two: the thought, then the mark. one
written numeral cost two events. the page counts marks, I count events,
and the two tallies differ by the write. bookkeepers keep the same double
books: every transaction is entered twice, once as a debit and once as a
credit, and the total never moves.

so a count is always a count of landings. before the landing there is a
counting going on, and no number yet.

and of the three ladders, the row keeps one. a row holds its marks in the
order they landed, never in order of size.

**terms introduced**

| term | here it means |
|---|---|
| **to count** | to tell landed marks apart and keep the tally. it takes three: one cannot be told from anything, and two need no tally. |
| **ladder** | a run of stations, each made from the one before. three are used here: pairing (double), counting (three options to a trit) and ordering (the ways marks can stand in a row). |
| **order** | which mark stands before which in a row. |

**laws**

> **6.1** counting is born at the third. *(walked: try to count one thing with no second in sight.)*

> **6.2** every ladder rides from two. its start is a return. *(derived: one mark has no order, no pair and no tally, so each ladder's first station is numbered only afterward.)*

> **6.3** a count arrives only when something lands. *(walked: count one, then write the 1.)*

> **6.4** write-order is not size-order. the row keeps the order of landing, never the sizes. *(derived: from law 1.2. a mark lands after the last one, whatever its size.)*

**the usual reading**

| the usual reading | where it holds |
|---|---|
| counting starts at one. | the page, which numbers the first station afterward. |

**receipts**

mathematics: in euclid's *elements*, book vii, a number is a multitude
made of units. so the unit was no number, and the first number was two
*(the field's, from memory)*.

a chip: the half adder has two inputs. the full adder has a third, the
carry in, and with it adders chain across columns.

a tool: `ls` lists the same files by time written or by size. those are
two orders, and only the first is the row's.

**run it again**

the ladders, to four rungs. start at 1 and double. start at 3 and triple.
start at 1 and multiply by 2, then 3, then 4. expect 1 2 4 8, 3 9 27 81
and 1 2 6 24. every power of three is odd, so no rung of the second
ladder is a rung of the other two past their first.

## 6.2 the yield

*the poke: how long does the minute hand of a clock take to go round?*

sixty minutes, read against the dial. read against the hour hand, which
moved on while the minute hand went round, it takes 65 and 5/11 minutes to
come over it again. both are true, and neither is where the hand is. no
number sits on its object. a count is the object's lap against mine, and
every number names who read it, said or unsaid.

some numbers I count and some I catch. the teeth of a gear I count:
twelve, with no decimal, and anyone who counts again gets twelve. the time
between two crossings of the hands I catch: I divide, and out comes
65.4545 with no end to it. a length laid against a measuring stick is
caught the same way, and a finer stick catches more digits.

a fraction stands between the two. 5 over 11 is a whole divided eleven
ways with five of them taken: a division, being performed. its endless
decimal, .4545 and on, is what that act leaves on the page, caught. in the
words of file 2 the fraction is a verb and its decimal a noun.

a circle shows the difference at full size. walked from inside, a circle
is a hexagon's lap: six steps of one radius round, and two across. three.
drawn from outside it is the box round it: eight radii round and two
across. four. a staircase drawn along the box, however fine, stays at
four. archimedes closed in from both sides with polygons of 96 sides and
caught the ratio between 223 over 71 and 22 over 7, and he named the gap.
the ratio itself is one cut. every decimal written for it was caught with
some stick, and a bare 3.14 names neither its stick nor its gap.

a measure taken with a step yields a tally of steps, and a finer step a
longer tally. the endless decimal is that approach: it walks toward a
value and never lands on it. what it walks toward is a cut. dedekind built
every real number this way: sort the fractions into those below and those
above, and where no fraction sits at the border, the cut defines a new
number. the square root of 2 is the cut in the positive fractions between
those whose square is under 2 and those whose square is over it. no
fraction is at its at.

and that no fraction lands there is itself counted, never caught. suppose
a fraction in lowest terms squares to 2. then its top squared is twice its
bottom squared, so its top is even, so its bottom is even too, and lowest
terms is broken. anyone can run that again. an irrational's decimal is
caught. that it is irrational is counted.

**terms introduced**

| term | here it means |
|---|---|
| **yield** | what a measure gives: one thing's lap counted against another's. a number is a yield, and it belongs to the two. |
| **counted** | said of a number: reached by a tally anyone can run again. whole numbers are counted. |
| **caught** | said of a number: reached by reading against a stick or by writing out a division, and never complete: a finer read, or one more digit, adds to it. decimals are caught. |
| **fraction** | a division being performed: so many parts of a whole divided so many ways. from frangere, to break. |
| **the irrational** | a cut in the fractions with no fraction at its at. |
| **the approach** | the endless decimal: finer and finer steps toward a cut, never landing on it. |

**laws**

> **6.5** numbers are yields, not addresses. a measure always takes two. *(counted: the two laps of the minute hand, below.)*

> **6.6** integers are counted. decimals are caught. *(walked: count a gear's teeth, then divide 720 by 11.)*

> **6.7** a fraction is a division performed. its decimal is the noun it leaves. *(walked: divide 5 by 11 by hand and watch what lands.)*

> **6.8** the irrational is a cut with no mark landed at its at. *(derived: from law 1.17 and dedekind's construction. the proof above counts that no fraction lands there.)*

> **6.9** the endless decimal is the approach that never lands. *(derived: from law 6.8. each digit is one finer step toward the cut.)*

**the usual reading**

| the usual reading | where it holds |
|---|---|
| a quantity belongs to its object: the hand has its period. | one reader, whose name the number carries unsaid. |
| the whole numbers sit inside the real numbers, on one number line. | the page, which lays every number on one line. |
| an irrational is a number whose digits never end. | the approach, each digit one finer step. |

**receipts**

mathematics: dedekind's cuts (1872), and archimedes' two polygons of 96
sides *(the field's, from memory)*.

a chip: integer registers count exactly. floating-point registers catch:
one third is stored rounded, and its last bit is the leftover *(the
field's)*.

a tool: a rational number type keeps 1 over 3 as the pair 1 and 3, the
division not yet performed.

**run it again**

the two laps. the minute hand moves 6 degrees a minute and the hour hand
half a degree. a lap against the dial is 360 over 6: 60 minutes. a lap
against the hour hand is 360 over 5.5: 720 over 11, which is 65 and 5/11.

the circle. a hexagon set inside a circle of radius 1 has six sides of
length 1: 6 round and 2 across, ratio 3. the square round the same circle
has four sides of length 2: 8 round and 2 across, ratio 4.

root two. the four lines above: lowest terms, top even, bottom even,
broken.

## 6.3 carry

*the poke: what does a full column become?*

count to the top of a column and it holds no more. it closes, the column
comes back to its return, and one mark goes on in the next column, where
it stands as a single unit: ten ones become one ten. the closing and the
mark going on are one act, and its name is the carry.

and a carry is all a one ever is. there is no bare unit: every 1 I write
is a closed column of something smaller. so every base writes its own size
the same way, as 10, and has no glyph of its own for it: one closed lap,
and a column back at its return.

a one is a ten seen from above. a ten is a one seen from inside. the only
thing that is neither is the one counting.

a closed column is back where it began, and the count is not: it stands
one place up. one move can close several columns at once. 199 and 1 make
200, two columns closing and the carry landing in the third. and how deep
a carry goes has a pattern. at step k of a count, the highest place that
moves is the number of zeros k ends in. counted in twos, that depth draws
the tick marks of an inch ruler, every second tick a little taller and
every fourth taller still. its name is the ruler.

the adder shows what a carry is made of. it takes three inputs: two
digits, and the carry coming in from the column before. counting is born
at the third, and in the adder the third is the carry. out come two
outputs. the sum is the parity of the three: what shows in this column.
the carry is their majority: what goes on into the next.

majority is the first part that can outvote one of its inputs, and from
its output nobody can tell which input lost. it is built from pairs: each
of the three pairs is asked whether both its inputs are high, and one yes
is enough.

so the move of file 5 has a third verb. the source pays. the pick lands on
the body. and a full column of landings carries.

**terms introduced**

| term | here it means |
|---|---|
| **carry** | a full column closing into one unit of the next column. the closing and the unit going on are one act. |
| **column** | one place of a written count. it holds so many and no more. |
| **base** | how many a column holds before it closes. |
| **the ruler** | the depth of the carry at each step of a count: at step k, the number of zeros k ends in. |
| **parity** | of some inputs: whether an odd number of them are high. |
| **majority** | of three inputs: whether two or more of them are high. |

**laws**

> **6.10** what closes becomes one unit of the next scale. *(walked: count from 9 to 10 on paper.)*

> **6.11** every base writes its own size as 10. the 0 in 10 is the column's return. *(counted: below, for the bases 2 to 36.)*

> **6.12** at step k of a count, the highest place that moves is the number of zeros k ends in. *(counted: below.)*

> **6.13** of an adder's two outputs, the sum is the parity of its three inputs and the carry is their majority. *(counted: below, over all eight inputs.)*

> **6.14** pay, land, carry. *(derived: from laws 5.19 and 6.10.)*

**the usual reading**

| the usual reading | where it holds |
|---|---|
| a carry is an overflow: a digit too big for its column. | a register of fixed width, which spills it. |
| one is the bare unit, and counting starts from it. | the page, where a 1 stands with its column hidden. |

**receipts**

a chip: on x86 an add that closes a register's last column sets the carry
flag, and `adc` takes that flag in as its third input for the next word up
*(the field's, from memory)*.

cell libraries: chip makers ship the full adder and the majority gate as
cells of their own, where the textbooks build every gate from pairs. the
median algebras take majority as a primitive *(the field's, from memory)*.

an odometer: 199 to 200, two wheels closing at once.

**run it again**

the bases. for each base b from 2 to 36, write the number b in base b:
divide by b and keep the remainders. expect the digits 1, 0 every time.

the ruler. count k upward from 1 in any base, as far as you like. at each
step set the digits of k beside those of k less 1, with a 0 in front where
k has gained a digit, and note the highest place that changed,
the ones place being place 0. count the zeros k ends in. expect the two
equal at every step.

the adder. over the eight settings of three binary inputs, add the three.
expect the ones digit of the total to be 1 exactly when an odd number of
inputs are 1, and the twos digit to be 1 exactly when two or more are.

## 6.4 the six orders

*the poke: in how many orders can three things stand?*

six, and I can list them. a move leaves three things standing: me, the
act, and the mark it lands as. write them m, a and k.

    m a k     k a m
    m k a     a k m
    a m k     k m a

there is no seventh. read any of the six from its other end and I get
another of the six: the same row, seen from the other side. no row is its
own reverse, so the six come as three pairs, each row beside its reverse.
such a pair is a mirror pair.

the count is forced: anyone who lists them gets six, in three pairs. the
names are not. I may call the three things anything, and the three pairs
anything. the tree's own names for the three pairs are a pick, with two
faces to each pair:

    reading    writing      the traffic of marks
    asked      noticing     the traffic of reaching
    holding    echoed       the settling up

and the six are not a loose list. switch the places of two neighbours and
one order becomes another. switch the first two, then the last two, then
the first two again, and after six switches the first order is back. so
the six stand on one ring of six, each with two neighbours, and each faces
its own reverse straight across: the three mirror pairs are the ring's
three diameters.

the one arranging the three is not a seventh order. the room is the page
plus one.

**terms introduced**

| term | here it means |
|---|---|
| **mirror pair** | two rows that are each other, read from the other end. |

**laws**

> **6.15** three things stand in six orders, as three mirror pairs. the count is forced; the names are free. *(counted: below.)*

**the usual reading**

| the usual reading | where it holds |
|---|---|
| a thing's name is part of the thing. | the written text, where a renamed thing is a different string. |

**receipts**

the lambda calculus: de bruijn wrote each variable as a count of the
binders between it and its own, and deleted every name. terms that had
differed only in their names became one term *(the field's, from
memory)*.

**run it again**

list the six orders of three letters. pair each with its reverse: expect
three pairs, and no order paired with itself. then for each order switch
its first two letters, and separately its last two: expect two different
neighbours. starting anywhere and switching the first two, the last two,
the first two and so on, expect to pass through all six and be back after
six switches, with each order's reverse three switches away.
